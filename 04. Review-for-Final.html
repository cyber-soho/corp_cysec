A note on ethics and the law (read before the offensive chapters)

Several chapters in this program — vulnerability management, penetration testing, incident handling — teach offensive and investigative techniques. Those skills are taught for one reason: to defend systems you are authorized to defend. Running any scan, capture, exploit, or social-engineering exercise outside a lab you own, or outside a signed scope of work, is a criminal act in essentially every jurisdiction this program's students live and work in.

Before you touch a tool, you accept that unauthorized access, interception, or interference with computer data may be prosecuted under, among others, the Criminal Code of Canada, section 342.1 (unauthorized use of computer) — laws-lois.justice.gc.ca; the United States Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030 — law.cornell.edu; the European Union Directive 2013/40/EU on attacks against information systems — eur-lex.europa.eu; and Spain's Ley Orgánica 10/1995 del Código Penal — boe.es. Local statutes apply on top of these.

Chapter 1 — Preventative Monitoring

420-1C1-DW · Competency EZ01 · 75 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: monitor a computer network from a cybersecurity perspective.

This is the foundation stone. Everything else in the program leans on it. The course starts with the idea every security professional carries around for the rest of their career: the CIA triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability). Confidentiality means only the right people can read the data. Integrity means the data has not been tampered with and you can prove it. Availability means the system is there when a legitimate user needs it. A single incident can attack one corner of the triangle or all three at once, and almost every control you will ever deploy exists to protect one of these three properties. A useful counterpoint the course raises early is the difference between a security problem and a performance problem — a server that is slow because it is overloaded is not necessarily under attack, and confusing the two wastes everyone's time.

From there the course catalogues the attacks you are defending against — phishing, malware in its many shapes, and DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) floods among them — and pairs them with the defensive philosophies that answer them. Three of those philosophies recur across the whole program. Defense in depth layers independent controls so that one failure does not become a breach. Zero Trust drops the old assumption that anything inside the perimeter is automatically trustworthy and instead verifies every request. Least privilege gives each account, process, and person exactly the access they need and nothing more.

Next comes topology. You cannot watch a network you cannot picture, so the course has you diagram star, mesh, and hybrid layouts (the classroom tool was Draw.io, though any diagramming tool works) and map live hosts with Nmap. Once you can see the network, you weigh its organizational and operational constraints — budget, staff, uptime requirements, regulatory pressure — and decide what actually needs watching and in what order. That prioritisation feeds a formal risk assessment, the same discipline you will meet again, more rigorously, in Chapter 2.

The back half of the course is hands-on surveillance. You survey the monitoring landscape — Nagios, Zabbix, and Splunk are the named examples — choose tools against explicit selection criteria, install and configure them, and set up alerts and thresholds so the system tells you when something is wrong instead of making you go look. You learn to maintain and update those tools and to build a reference database of known-good baselines, because anomaly detection is meaningless without a definition of "normal." The final movements are detection and diagnosis (using Wireshark to read traffic and host-based detection such as OSSEC to spot tampering), optimisation so monitoring does not itself drag the network down, and reporting — turning what you found into something a stakeholder can act on, often through the ELK Stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana) from Elastic.

Exam focus — Chapter 1. Be able to define each letter of the CIA triad and give an attack that violates it. Distinguish defense in depth, Zero Trust, and least privilege. Know why you baseline before you alert. Match a tool category (network monitor, packet analyser, host-based detector, log/SIEM pipeline) to its job rather than memorising vendors.

Chapter 2 — Threats and Risks

420-1C2-DW · Competency EZ02 · 75 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: risk and threat analysis.

If Chapter 1 taught you to watch, Chapter 2 teaches you to reason about what could go wrong and how badly. It opens with information assets — the things you are actually protecting. Assets come in four flavours the course drills repeatedly: hardware, software, data, and services. You inventory them (the lab used GLPI for asset management) and attach permissions to them, governed by RBAC (Role-Based Access Control), where access follows job role rather than individual whim. Identity tooling such as FreeIPA ties those roles to real accounts.

Then the threats. The course splits them cleanly into internal (the disgruntled employee, the careless click, the contractor with too much access) and external (organised crime, hacktivists, nation-state actors, opportunists), and asks you to profile the actor — their capability and, crucially, their motivation — because a bored teenager and a funded espionage group warrant very different defences. Profiling leans on OSINT (Open Source Intelligence): everything an adversary (or a defender) can learn from public sources. You practised gathering it with SpiderFoot, Recon-ng, and Maltego, and you should understand that the same techniques the attacker uses to map you, you use to understand your own exposure.

The analytical heart of the course is risk itself. A risk factor has two dimensions you must keep separate: probability (how likely is exploitation) and impact/severity (how much it hurts if it happens). Multiply, weigh, or matrix them and you get a score that lets you rank one risk against another. This is where the formal frameworks enter, and they matter for the exam: the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) risk guidance, particularly SP 800-30 — csrc.nist.gov; the international standard ISO 31000 for risk management — iso.org; and FAIR (Factor Analysis of Information Risk), which expresses risk in financial terms — fairinstitute.org. Tooling such as OpenVAS for assessment and OWASP Threat Dragon for threat modelling turns the theory into artefacts.

Finally you decide what to do about each risk. The four classic treatment options are the ones to memorise cold: avoidance (stop doing the risky thing), reduction/mitigation (add controls to lower probability or impact), sharing/transfer (insurance, outsourcing), and acceptance (consciously living with it because treatment costs more than the risk). Beyond treatment, the course covers eradication of active threats, preventative best practice, and BC/DR (Business Continuity / Disaster Recovery) — keeping the business running through a disruption and getting it back to normal afterwards, practised with high-availability tooling like Keepalived and HAProxy. A standing caution the course repeats: every preventative measure carries its own risk, so you weigh the cure as carefully as the disease.

Exam focus — Chapter 2. Keep probability and impact as two separate axes. Name the four risk-treatment options and give an example of each. Know which framework does what (ISO 31000 = process, NIST SP 800-30 = assessment method, FAIR = dollars). Distinguish internal vs external threats and explain why attacker motivation changes your response.

Chapter 3 — Cyber Defense

420-2C1-DW · Competency EZ03 · 90 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: application of access control measures.

This is where defending stops being theory and becomes configuration. The course is built around layered, active defence and splits naturally into four jobs: defend the perimeter, control access to hosts, control access to networks, and apply cryptography.

Perimeter and segmentation come first. You start at the host firewall level with Linux iptables (chains, tables, rules, and stateful inspection — the firewall remembers the state of a connection rather than judging each packet in isolation) and its friendlier front end UFW (Uncomplicated Firewall). You then graduate to dedicated firewall distributions: OPNsense, with its VLANs (Virtual Local Area Networks) for carving one physical network into isolated segments, VPN (Virtual Private Network) basics, and NAT (Network Address Translation) rules; and IPFire with its colour-coded zone model — Red for the untrusted outside, Green for the trusted inside, Blue for wireless, Orange for a DMZ (Demilitarized Zone) where internet-facing services live. The single most important idea here is segmentation: if every zone can reach every other zone, a foothold anywhere becomes a breach everywhere. Firewall rules between segments are what stop lateral movement.

Host hardening is the second job. The principle is least privilege again, made concrete: change the default SSH (Secure Shell) port, disable root login, manage sudo privileges carefully, switch off services you do not use, and lock down files with Linux permissions (chmod, chown) and ACLs (Access Control Lists) for finer-grained control. Automated protection such as Fail2Ban watches authentication logs and bans the addresses behind brute-force attempts. Every hardening step you apply, you then validate — an untested control is a hope, not a defence.

Detection is the third job. You meet IDS/IPS (Intrusion Detection System / Intrusion Prevention System) concepts and the difference between signature detection (matches known-bad patterns) and anomaly detection (flags deviations from normal). Security Onion bundles network sensors — Suricata for signature-based NIDS (Network Intrusion Detection System) and NSM (Network Security Monitoring), and Zeek for rich traffic metadata. On the host side, Wazuh acts as a HIDS (Host-based Intrusion Detection System) with SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) capabilities, doing log analysis, alert correlation, FIM (File Integrity Monitoring), and active response. You also deploy deception: honeypots like OpenCanary and Cowrie (a medium-interaction SSH/Telnet trap) that exist to be attacked, so the only traffic they ever see is hostile and therefore high-signal intelligence.

The fourth job is cryptography. You apply the basic concepts (the goals map straight back to the CIA triad: encryption for confidentiality, hashing and signatures for integrity), implement the main cryptosystems, survey the headline attacks against them, and — the part students underrate — learn to choose the right cryptographic technique for the application, securing both data at rest (stored) and data in transit (transitory). Symmetric ciphers are fast and suit bulk data; asymmetric (public-key) cryptography solves key exchange and enables signatures; hashing verifies integrity but never "encrypts." Knowing which to reach for, and why, is the assessable skill.

Exam focus — Chapter 3. Explain stateful inspection in one sentence. Justify network segmentation in terms of lateral movement. List concrete SSH-hardening steps. Separate signature from anomaly detection, and NIDS from HIDS. Say what a honeypot is for. Match a cryptographic primitive (symmetric, asymmetric, hash) to its purpose and to the CIA property it serves.

Host hardening Least privilege made real; validate every control
Detection Signature vs anomaly; NIDS (Suricata/Zeek) vs HIDS/SIEM (Wazuh)

Chapter 4 — Compliance Assessments

420-2C2-DW · Competency EZ04 · 45 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: assessment of compliance with policies and standards.

Compliance is the discipline of proving — to a regulator, a board, or a customer — that your controls actually meet a defined bar. The course frames the whole field with GRC (Governance, Risk, and Compliance) and a distinction students must nail: laws are passed by legislatures and are mandatory; regulations are issued by agencies to enforce laws; standards are agreed best-practice benchmarks (sometimes mandatory by contract); policies are the internal rules an organisation writes for itself. The course runs a mock company, "NorthStream," through a full assessment so the abstractions stay grounded.

The legal landscape is the largest block, and the exam expects you to know who each instrument governs. On the Canadian side: PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act) and its ten Fair Information Principles — priv.gc.ca; Quebec's Law 25, which modernised provincial privacy law and added real penalties — cai.gouv.qc.ca; and Bill C-26, aimed at protecting the cyber security of critical infrastructure — parl.ca. Internationally: the EU's GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), with its extraterritorial reach, data-subject rights, "right to be forgotten," and 72-hour breach-notification clock — gdpr.eu; and from the United States, HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) for health data — hhs.gov, SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley Act) for public-company financial reporting — sec.gov, and CCPA/CPRA (California Consumer Privacy Act / California Privacy Rights Act) — oag.ca.gov. When two regimes collide (GDPR is generally stricter than PIPEDA, for example), the stricter requirement usually wins, and cross-border data transfer raises questions of data sovereignty.

Laws tell you what; frameworks tell you how. The course covers the NIST CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) — nist.gov/cyberframework; ISO 27001 for information-security management systems — iso.org; and PCI-DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) for anyone handling card transactions — pcisecuritystandards.org. The real skill is mapping: taking a legal requirement and pointing to the specific framework control that satisfies it. Below the framework sit hardening benchmarks — CIS (Center for Internet Security) Benchmarks — cisecurity.org — and STIGs (Security Technical Implementation Guides) from the US Defense Information Systems Agency — public.cyber.mil — together with the idea of a configuration baseline or "golden image."

The doing-it half covers audit scope (cloud vs on-premises boundaries), methodology (interview vs observation vs technical testing), and gap analysis (current state vs desired state). Automated evidence-gathering uses SCAP (Security Content Automation Protocol) via OpenSCAP and scanners such as Nessus/OpenVAS run in compliance mode rather than vulnerability mode, plus scripting in PowerShell and Python to verify versions, patch levels, and log coverage. Supply-chain assurance leans on SOC 2 Type II reports — and the course specifically teaches you to hunt for the exclusion clauses in a vendor's report that quietly leave you exposed. Everything lands in a written compliance report: a neutral, fact-based executive summary for the board plus a technical annex for the engineers, with findings scored by impact × likelihood and remediation prioritised by business impact. When you genuinely cannot meet a control, you document a compensating control instead.

Exam focus — Chapter 4. Cleanly separate law / regulation / standard / policy. Match each named law to the data or sector it governs and recall the GDPR 72-hour rule. Know the difference between a framework (NIST CSF, ISO 27001, PCI-DSS) and a benchmark (CIS, STIG). Explain why you map legal requirements to technical controls, and what a SOC 2 exclusion clause and a compensating control are.

Chapter 5 — Managing Vulnerabilities

420-3C1-DW · Competency EZ05 · 60 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: manage software vulnerabilities.

This course moves you from reacting to attacks to anticipating them. It fuses two disciplines: the VM (Vulnerability Management) lifecycle and CTI (Cyber Threat Intelligence). The lifecycle is a loop — discover assets, analyse the attack surface, scan for weaknesses, score and prioritise, remediate, and verify, then start again — and CTI is what tells you which of your thousand findings actually matter this week because real adversaries are exploiting them right now.

Threat intelligence starts with sources. You learn to read CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) records — the shared dictionary of publicly known flaws — cve.org; watch alerts from CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) — cisa.gov; and fold in OSINT feeds. You then place those threats inside analytical models. The Cyber Kill Chain, from Lockheed Martin, breaks an intrusion into ordered stages from reconnaissance to actions-on-objectives — lockheedmartin.com; the Diamond Model relates adversary, capability, infrastructure, and victim — activeresponse.org; and MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) catalogues the concrete tactics and techniques attackers actually use, so you can map a given CVE to the behaviours that would exploit it — attack.mitre.org. Mapping a vulnerability to a framework is exactly how you turn "there is a flaw" into "here is the path an attacker would take, so here is what to watch and patch first."

Then the mechanics. You define the attack surface (everything an attacker could reach), build an asset inventory with discovery tools like Nmap, and write a vulnerability-scan policy — scope and schedule — under the same regulatory pressures from earlier courses (PCI-DSS, HIPAA, GDPR all dictate scanning cadence). You distinguish scanner types: network scanners that probe from outside versus agent-based scanners that run on the host, and authenticated scans (with credentials, deeper and more accurate) versus unauthenticated scans (the attacker's-eye view). OpenVAS and Nessus are the worked examples; tuning scan profiles and sensitivity so you neither miss real issues nor drown in noise is a graded skill.

Scoring is the analytical core, and it is the most exam-tested topic in the course: the CVSS (Common Vulnerability Scoring System), maintained by FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams) — first.org/cvss. CVSS has three metric groups, and you must keep them straight. The Base score describes the intrinsic, unchanging characteristics of the flaw. The Temporal score adjusts for factors that change over time (is exploit code available, is a patch out). The Environmental score tailors the result to your specific deployment — a critical flaw on an isolated test box is not the same risk as the same flaw on an internet-facing database. A raw base score is only the starting point; contextualising it is the job.

Application and web vulnerabilities get their own treatment through the OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) Top 10 — owasp.org/www-project-top-ten — and tools like OWASP ZAP (Zed Attack Proxy) for scanning web apps, alongside the management of third-party software risk. The loop closes with patch management — a disciplined lifecycle of testing patches before deployment and verifying remediation afterward, and understanding that remediation (fix the flaw) and mitigation (reduce exposure without fully fixing) are different answers. Finally, threat hunting flips the posture to proactive: forming a hypothesis and searching for IoCs (Indicators of Compromise) — using YARA rules and log searches — to find evidence that an unpatched weakness has already been used against you.

Exam focus — Chapter 5. Recite the VM lifecycle as a loop. Place the three CVSS metric groups (Base / Temporal / Environmental) and say what each captures. Separate network vs agent and authenticated vs unauthenticated scans. Distinguish remediation from mitigation. Know what the Cyber Kill Chain, Diamond Model, and MITRE ATT&CK each model, and what an IoC is.

Chapter 6 — Intrusion Tests

420-3C2-DW · Competency EZ06 · 90 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: perform penetration (hacking) tests — ethically and under authorization.

This is the offensive course, and it opens, deliberately, with ethics and law rather than tools. A penetration test is legitimate only because of paperwork: RoE (Rules of Engagement) and a precisely defined scope that say exactly what may be touched, when, and how. Test outside that scope and you are no longer a tester — you are a criminal under the statutes listed at the top of this review. The course frames its methodology against recognised standards: PTES (Penetration Testing Execution Standard) and the CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker) phase model, both of which structure an engagement into ordered phases.

Before attacking anything, the course spends real time on OPSEC (Operational Security) — protecting the tester. You build a layered anonymity architecture (firewall plus chained VPNs, the isolation of Whonix, and the compartmentalisation of Qubes OS) and you stand up an isolated lab: an attack platform such as Kali Linux or Parrot OS, and deliberately vulnerable targets — Metasploitable, DVWA (Damn Vulnerable Web Application), and OWASP Juice Shop. Snapshots and network segmentation are validated before any offensive action, every time.

The engagement then walks the phases. Footprinting and OSINT is passive reconnaissance — DNS and WHOIS lookups, subdomain discovery, breach data, metadata — using theHarvester, Amass, Shodan, and Maltego against safe targets. Social engineering covers phishing and smishing as simulations with measurable awareness goals, never live external victims. Vulnerability analysis reuses scanners and CVSS from Chapter 5 and maps findings to MITRE ATT&CK. Scanning and enumeration is active reconnaissance — TCP and UDP scans, service and version detection, and SMB/LDAP/SNMP enumeration with nmap, enum4linux, ldapsearch, and snmpwalk. Wireless work introduces the PMKID (Pairwise Master Key Identifier) attack as a methodology study.

The exploitation phases are taught conceptually and inside the lab only. System hacking covers credential handling (hashes and cracking), LPE (Local Privilege Escalation) on Linux and Windows with enumeration helpers like LinPEAS/WinPEAS, and the discipline of clean-up. Sniffing and MITM (Man-in-the-Middle) covers ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) spoofing and PCAP analysis in Wireshark, using tools like bettercap against lab logins. Web applications and APIs revisits the OWASP Top 10 hands-on — SQLi (SQL Injection), XSS (Cross-Site Scripting), IDOR (Insecure Direct Object Reference), and SSRF (Server-Side Request Forgery) — through Burp Suite and sqlmap. Active Directory and lateral movement introduces Kerberos abuse — AS-REP roasting and Kerberoasting — and PtH (Pass-the-Hash), mapped with BloodHound and exercised with Impacket against safe accounts. The course closes on IDS/firewall evasion and tunnelling (decoys, fragmentation, timing, proxychains) and the ethics of DoS (Denial of Service) concepts.

Every engagement ends the same way it began — with a document. The deliverable is a report that states the methodology and its limitations, scores severity honestly, presents results an executive can act on, and prioritises fixes. A test that finds everything but explains nothing is a failed test.

Exam focus — Chapter 6. Define RoE and scope and explain why they make a test lawful. Order the engagement phases (recon → scanning/enumeration → exploitation → post-exploitation → reporting). Separate passive from active reconnaissance. Match an attack name (SQLi, XSS, IDOR, Kerberoasting, ARP spoofing, PtH) to what it abuses. Remember that the report — methodology, limitations, severity, fixes — is the real product.

Chapter 7 — Incident Management

420-4C1-DW · Competency EZ07 · 75 hours

 Fold

One-line competency: manage security incidents.

The final course assumes the worst has happened and teaches you to handle it without making it worse. Its backbone is the IH&R (Incident Handling and Response) process — a repeatable lifecycle that turns panic into procedure. The phases the course works through are preparation, detection and validation, containment, eradication, recovery, and the lessons-learned follow-up. Two ideas anchor everything: you cannot improvise a response (preparation is a phase, not an afterthought), and you must validate that an alert is a real incident before you tear systems offline over a false positive.

First response and evidence handling is the phase students most often underestimate. The moment you suspect an incident, you are also handling potential evidence, which means chain of custody — a documented, unbroken record of who handled each artefact, when, and why — and careful evidence preservation so that volatile data (memory, live connections) is captured before it vanishes and disk images are taken in a forensically sound way. Mishandled evidence is not just lost intelligence; it can be inadmissible if the incident ever reaches a courtroom or a regulator. This is also where the breach-notification clocks from Chapter 4 start ticking — GDPR's 72 hours, Canadian record-keeping rules — so the clock-management and the forensics happen in parallel.

The course then walks the major incident categories, because the response differs by type. Malware incidents require detection, analysis, and containment that isolates the infection before it spreads. Email security incidents — phishing and spam — are traced by reading email headers to follow the path a message actually took. Web application incidents revisit the OWASP weaknesses from the offensive courses, now from the defender's side. Network incidents — DoS floods and unauthorized access — are validated through traffic analysis. Insider threats demand detection and eradication of activity by someone who already has legitimate access, which is harder precisely because they are supposed to be there. Endpoint incidents stretch the perimeter to mobile, IoT (Internet of Things), and OT (Operational Technology) devices, each with its own forensic quirks and, in the case of OT, safety consequences that pure IT incidents rarely carry. Tooling spans forensic suites for evidence gathering and the monitoring and EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) stacks you met earlier.

The course also closes the loop back to the very first competency in the program: raising user awareness. A handler who eradicates an incident but never tells anyone how it happened guarantees a repeat. So the deliverables include a detailed incident report (what happened, what was done, what it cost, what changes are recommended) and an awareness component — relevant threats, demonstrated impact, real case studies, and concrete recommendations for safer behaviour. Notice how this folds the whole program together: you monitor (Chapter 1), you reason about risk (Chapter 2), you defend (Chapter 3), you prove compliance (Chapter 4), you manage weaknesses (Chapter 5), you test offensively to find them first (Chapter 6), and when something still gets through, you respond and you teach (Chapter 7).

Exam focus — Chapter 7. Order the IH&R lifecycle and stress that preparation and validation come before containment. Define chain of custody and explain why evidence handling matters legally, not just technically. Match an incident category (malware, phishing, network, insider, endpoint/OT) to how its response differs. Connect breach-notification deadlines back to Chapter 4. Know why user awareness is part of incident management, not separate from it.

How the seven chapters connect (one picture for the exam)

The program is a single story told in two arcs. The defensive arc — Monitoring, Threats & Risks, Cyber Defense, Compliance, Vulnerabilities — builds the wall and proves it stands. The offensive arc — Intrusion Tests — attacks your own wall to find the gaps before a real adversary does. Incident Management is what happens on the day the wall is breached anyway. The CIA triad runs underneath all of it; risk prioritisation decides where you spend effort; documentation and communication turn every phase into something an organisation can actually use. If an exam question feels like it spans two courses, that is by design — the courses were never really separate.

Case 01

The 3 a.m. spike

Chapters 1, 7

Scenario. Plateau Realty's new monitoring stack pages the on-call technician at 03:00. A workstation in the accounting office is sending steady outbound traffic to an unfamiliar address, at a volume it has never produced before, every night that week. Nothing looks broken; users are asleep; the helpdesk has no tickets.

Questions. 1. Which property of the CIA triad is most directly threatened, and why? 2. What earlier step made this detection possible at all? 3. What should the technician do before pulling the machine off the network? 4. Is this definitely an incident? How do you decide?

Model answer. The pattern most directly threatens confidentiality — sustained, unexplained outbound traffic from a host holding financial data is the classic shape of data exfiltration, though availability and integrity could follow. The detection was only possible because someone had previously baselined "normal" for that host; an alert on "more traffic than usual" is meaningless without a reference of what usual looks like. Before disconnecting, the technician should validate the alert and preserve volatile evidence — capture the active network connections and, ideally, a memory snapshot — because yanking the cable destroys exactly the live data that would reveal where the traffic is going and what process is sending it. Whether it is a true incident is a judgement call made by validation, not assumption: a nightly cloud backup or a software update can mimic exfiltration, so you confirm the destination, the responsible process, and the business justification before escalating. This is the IH&R sequence in miniature — detect, validate, then contain.

Case 02

The resignation and the USB stick

Chapters 2, 7

Scenario. A Plateau Realty agent gives notice. In their final two weeks, audit logs show them downloading the full client database to a personal device and emailing several listing files to an outside address. They have legitimate access to all of it — it is their job.

Questions. 1. Internal or external threat? What makes insiders harder to catch? 2. How would you have profiled this risk before it happened? 3. Which risk-treatment options apply now, and which should have applied earlier? 4. What evidence-handling concern arises the moment you suspect wrongdoing?

Model answer. This is an internal threat, and insiders are harder to detect precisely because their actions sit inside their normal permissions — no firewall blocks them, no login fails, the behaviour only looks wrong in aggregate and context (bulk download plus external email plus an imminent departure). Profiling beforehand means recognising the motivation-plus-opportunity pattern: departing employees with broad data access are a known elevated-risk category, which is an argument for least privilege and for tightening access during notice periods. Among the four treatments, reduction should have applied earlier (limit bulk export, alert on large downloads, restrict personal devices), while right now the live options are reduction/containment (revoke access immediately) and possibly acceptance of residual exposure for data already gone, with legal sharing/transfer considerations if contracts or insurance are involved. The instant you suspect wrongdoing, chain of custody begins — the logs and any recovered device become potential evidence, so they must be preserved in a documented, tamper-evident way in case the matter becomes a legal or regulatory one.

Case 03

One flat network

Chapters 3, 1

Scenario. Plateau Realty grew fast and never segmented. Reception kiosks, the accounting servers, the public-facing booking website, the office printers, and the staff laptops all sit on one subnet. A consultant warns that this is the single biggest weakness in the building.

Questions. 1. Why is a flat network so dangerous in attacker terms? 2. Sketch a segmentation design using a zone model. 3. Which host-hardening steps would you prioritise on the accounting server? 4. After applying controls, what must you not skip?

Model answer. A flat network is dangerous because it removes all friction from lateral movement — a foothold on the least-important device (a kiosk, a printer) sits on the same segment as the crown-jewel accounting server, so one compromise is effectively a full compromise. A sound design borrows IPFire's zone thinking: put the booking website in a DMZ (Demilitarized Zone) isolated from the internal network, keep accounting on a tightly controlled internal segment, place kiosks and guest devices on a separate low-trust segment, and write firewall rules between segments so traffic only flows where business requires. On the accounting server, prioritise least-privilege hardening: disable unnecessary services, restrict and key-protect remote access (change defaults, disable root login, control sudo), apply strict file permissions and ACLs (Access Control Lists), and add brute-force protection on authentication. The step never to skip is validation — every control is tested to confirm it actually does what you intended, because an unverified firewall rule or a typo'd permission is a false sense of security, not a defence.

Case 04

NorthStream goes cross-border

Chapters 4, 2

Scenario. NorthStream, a Quebec company, signs European customers and begins storing their personal data in a US-based cloud region. The board asks the compliance team a blunt question: "Which laws do we have to follow now, and what is the worst-case clock if we get breached?"

Questions. 1. Which legal instruments now apply, and to which data? 2. When two regimes conflict, which requirement governs? 3. What is the breach-notification deadline the board is most exposed to? 4. How do you turn these legal duties into something the engineers can implement?

Model answer. Storing Quebec residents' data invokes PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act) and Quebec's Law 25; serving European customers invokes the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) because of its extraterritorial reach; and placing data in a US region raises data-sovereignty and cross-border-transfer questions, with US instruments like HIPAA or SOX applying only if the data type or company structure triggers them. Where regimes conflict, the stricter requirement generally governs — GDPR is typically stricter than PIPEDA, so designing to GDPR tends to satisfy both. The clock the board is most exposed to is GDPR's 72-hour breach-notification window, alongside Canadian record-keeping obligations that run in parallel. You translate duty into action through mapping: take each legal requirement and point it at a specific control in a framework such as the NIST CSF (Cybersecurity Framework) or ISO 27001, then implement and evidence that control — so "GDPR requires breach notification within 72 hours" becomes a documented, tested incident-response runbook with defined detection, escalation, and reporting timing.

Case 05

Same flaw, two servers

Chapters 5, 4

Scenario. A vulnerability scan at Plateau Realty flags the same critical software flaw on two machines: an internet-facing booking server and an isolated lab box used only for internal testing with no inbound access. Both show an identical CVSS base score of 9.1. The small team can only patch one immediately and wants to know which.

Questions. 1. If the base scores are identical, why are these not equal risks? 2. Which CVSS metric group resolves the question? 3. What is the difference between remediation and mitigation here? 4. What process discipline protects you from the patch itself?

Model answer. Identical base scores do not mean identical risk, because the base score captures only the flaw's intrinsic characteristics, not where it lives. The metric group that resolves the tie is the Environmental group, which tailors the score to your deployment: the internet-facing booking server is reachable by any attacker and handles client data, so its environmental-adjusted risk is far higher than the isolated lab box that nothing can route to. Patch the booking server first. Remediation means applying the vendor patch to actually fix the flaw; mitigation means reducing exposure without fully fixing it — for the lab box you might mitigate by confirming its network isolation and scheduling the patch for a maintenance window, accepting low residual risk in the meantime. The discipline that protects you from the cure is patch management's test-before-deploy lifecycle: validate the patch in a test environment first, then deploy, then verify remediation with a re-scan, because an untested patch on a production booking server can cause the very outage you were trying to prevent.

Case 06

Hiring a hacker, legally

Chapters 6, 4

Scenario. Plateau Realty hires an external penetration tester to assess the booking platform before a big launch. The tester's first email does not ask about exploits — it asks the company to sign a document and define exactly which systems, IP ranges, and hours are in play, and to confirm that the booking site is genuinely theirs to test.

Questions. 1. What is that document, and why does the tester insist on it first? 2. Order the phases the engagement will follow. 3. The tester finds a serious flaw on day one. Should they keep going? Why might they pause? 4. What makes the final report good, beyond the list of findings?

Model answer. The document is the RoE (Rules of Engagement) together with a defined scope and written authorization, and the tester insists on it first because it is the only thing separating a lawful security assessment from a criminal offence under computer-misuse law — without signed scope, every packet they send is potentially illegal. The engagement follows ordered phases: reconnaissance (passive footprinting/OSINT, then active scanning and enumeration), vulnerability analysis, exploitation, post-exploitation (privilege escalation, lateral movement, evidence collection), and reporting. Finding a serious flaw on day one does not automatically mean charging ahead — a professional may pause to notify the client immediately if the flaw is critical enough that leaving it unreported during the test window would be negligent, and they always stay strictly inside scope even when an out-of-scope system looks temptingly vulnerable. A good report is more than a findings list: it documents methodology and its limitations, scores each issue's severity honestly, presents results an executive can understand, and prioritises remediation by business impact — the value is in turning discoveries into an actionable plan, not in the trophy count.

Case 07

Friday afternoon ransomware

Chapters 7, 3

Scenario. At 16:40 on a Friday, Plateau Realty staff report that shared files have been renamed and a ransom note has appeared on several screens. Some machines are still working; others are locked. The owner wants everything "turned back on" immediately so agents can finish the week.

Questions. 1. Walk the IH&R lifecycle for this incident. 2. Why is "turn it all back on now" the wrong first move? 3. What evidence concerns apply, and what notification clocks may be running? 4. What should happen after recovery so this does not repeat?

Model answer. The IH&R (Incident Handling and Response) lifecycle applies directly: you are past preparation and detection; you validate the scope (which hosts, which shares, what variant), then contain by isolating affected and at-risk machines from the network and from backups to stop the encryption spreading, then eradicate the malware and any persistence, then recover from known-good backups, and finally run lessons-learned. "Turn it all back on now" is the wrong first move because reconnecting locked or compromised machines lets the ransomware continue encrypting, can re-infect cleaned systems, and destroys evidence — containment must precede restoration. The evidence concern is chain of custody and forensic preservation: capture the ransom note, affected samples, and volatile data before wiping, because the incident may trigger breach-notification clocks (Quebec's Law 25, PIPEDA record-keeping, and GDPR's 72-hour window if any EU personal data is involved) and may end up before a regulator or insurer. After recovery, the lessons-learned phase feeds back into defence: patch the entry vector, tighten segmentation and backups (offline/immutable copies), and — critically — run a user-awareness session, since ransomware so often enters through a single clicked attachment.

Case 08

Too many alerts, or too few

Chapters 3, 5

Scenario. Plateau Realty's analyst is drowning. Their network intrusion-detection system fires hundreds of alerts a day, almost all false positives, so real warnings get buried. A colleague suggests "just turn the sensitivity down," while another wants to add a honeypot. The team needs a defensible plan.

Questions. 1. Explain the trade-off between signature and anomaly detection in this fatigue. 2. Why might lowering sensitivity be exactly the wrong fix? 3. How does a honeypot change the signal-to-noise problem? 4. How does CVSS context help prioritise which alerts matter?

Model answer. Signature detection matches known-bad patterns and tends to be precise but blind to novelty, while anomaly detection flags deviations from baseline and catches the unknown but generates more false positives — alert fatigue usually comes from poorly tuned anomaly rules or signatures firing on benign traffic. Lowering sensitivity wholesale is the wrong fix because it trades a noise problem for a blindness problem: you will miss real intrusions, which is far more costly than reviewing noise. The better path is tuning — suppress known-benign triggers, baseline more accurately, and correlate alerts rather than treating each in isolation (a SIEM like Wazuh helps here). A honeypot (OpenCanary, Cowrie) attacks the signal-to-noise problem from the other end: because nothing legitimate should ever touch it, every interaction is suspicious, so its alerts are inherently high-confidence and need almost no filtering. Finally, CVSS environmental context helps trier the remaining alerts by asking which assets are actually exposed and critical — an alert against the internet-facing booking server outranks the same alert against an isolated test box, so context, not raw count, drives the queue.

Case 09

The booking site gets poked

Chapters 5, 6

Scenario. Before launch, Plateau Realty wants the booking web application assessed. The team must decide how to scan it, and the assessment finds that a logged-in user can change a number in the address bar and view another customer's booking, and that one search box reflects raw input back into the page.

Questions. 1. Authenticated or unauthenticated scan — what does each show you here? 2. Name the two web weaknesses described and the standard that catalogues them. 3. Which tooling category fits a web-application assessment? 4. Once found, are these remediated or mitigated?

Model answer. An unauthenticated scan shows you what an anonymous outsider sees, while an authenticated scan (run with valid credentials) reaches the logged-in functionality where these two flaws live — so an authenticated scan is essential here, because the booking-record exposure only appears after login. The first weakness — changing an identifier in the URL to read another user's record — is IDOR (Insecure Direct Object Reference); the second — user input reflected unsanitised back into the page — is XSS (Cross-Site Scripting); both are catalogued in the OWASP (Open Worldwide Application Security Project) Top 10. The right tooling category is a web-application scanner / proxy such as OWASP ZAP or Burp Suite, which can crawl, intercept, and test request/response behaviour rather than a generic network scanner. These are remediated, not merely mitigated: IDOR is fixed by enforcing proper authorization checks server-side so a user can only access their own records, and XSS is fixed by validating and encoding output — a temporary mitigation (such as a web-application-firewall rule) can buy time, but the durable answer is to correct the application code and verify with a re-test.

Case 10

The whole program in one bad week

Chapters 2, 4, 7

Scenario. NorthStream suffers a layered incident: a finance clerk is phished and their credentials are used to access a system holding EU and Quebec customer data; separately, a networked building-automation controller (an OT device) starts behaving erratically during the same window. Leadership wants one coherent answer covering threats, law, and response.

Questions. 1. Classify the threats involved and the assets at risk. 2. Which legal and notification duties are triggered? 3. How does handling the OT device differ from handling the finance system? 4. What single program-wide lesson ties this together?

Model answer. The threats are mixed: an external actor exploiting a social-engineering vector (phishing) to obtain credentials, with a possible insider-like footprint once those credentials are abused, plus an endpoint/OT anomaly that may be related or coincidental — and the assets at risk are data (EU and Quebec personal information) and a service/OT system. Legally, EU data triggers GDPR (including its 72-hour notification clock), Quebec data triggers Law 25 and PIPEDA record-keeping, so notification duties are running on parallel timers from the moment the breach is confirmed. Handling the OT (Operational Technology) controller differs sharply from the finance system: OT carries physical-safety and availability consequences, so you cannot simply power it off or image it the way you would an IT host — containment must weigh safety, vendor constraints, and process continuity, and forensics are gathered more cautiously. The single program-wide lesson is integration: monitoring caught the anomaly (Chapter 1), risk and threat reasoning classified it (Chapter 2), defensive segmentation should have limited the blast radius (Chapter 3), compliance set the legal clocks (Chapter 4), vulnerability management and pen testing should have surfaced the weaknesses first (Chapters 5–6), and incident management ties it together with response, reporting, and a follow-up awareness push (Chapter 7) — the courses are facets of one job, not seven separate ones.
